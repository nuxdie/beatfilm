# Three Voids — video script

Nov 13, 2026 · @-

## About this script

This is a first-person video essay of about 9 minutes, written for your voice, roughly 1,350 spoken words. Lines in *italics* are what is on screen; everything else is narration.

The painting is "Art Block" by Daniel Conway (2022). Credit him on screen and in the description, and link his page.

## Cold open: the painting

*Black screen. Slow fade in on the painting, full frame. Hold for three seconds in silence.*

A woman stands on a hillside covered in orange poppies. There are mountains behind her, a lake, and tall white clouds. The wind is pulling her dress to the right.

*Slow push in toward the figure.*

She's holding a palette and a brush. And in front of her, where the rest of the view should be, there's a huge black circle.

*Cut to a detail: the brush tip against the black.*

The rest of the picture is really detailed, you can make out single petals. The circle is just flat black, like somebody cut a hole in the file.

When I first saw it I couldn't tell what she was doing. Is she painting black over a finished world? Or is the world the painting, and the black is the bit she hasn't got to yet?

*Title card: THREE VOIDS*

## Part 1: the title

*The artist's page. Highlight the title, then the description.*

The painting is by Daniel Conway, and it's called "Art Block". He made it after losing his creative drive during the pandemic. He says a blank canvas felt like "staring into a void", and that even the world around him had stopped inspiring him.

*Back to the painting, wide.*

So she isn't painting the circle. The circle is the canvas, and she's stuck in front of it.

At first I thought the landscape was a bit much. It's postcard pretty. After reading his note it made sense. She's standing in about the most paintable place there is, she has her back to it, and she's got nothing.

*Detail: the raised brush, not touching.*

The other thing I noticed is that a blank canvas is white, and he made it black. A white canvas at least looks like you could start on it. This looks like anything you put in would just disappear.

It's also kind of funny that the way he got out of the block was to paint the block.

## Part 2: where I found it

*Hard cut to Minecraft footage: walking down a wall of map art in the museum.*

I should say where I found it, because it wasn't on his page. It was in Minecraft.

*The wall: memes, a shrimp, a sailing ship, a Starry Night. Then the painting, rebuilt in blocks.*

This is the 2b2t museum. If you don't know 2b2t, it's the oldest anarchy server in Minecraft. It's been the same world since December 2010, it has never been reset, and there are basically no rules. You can cheat, you can grief, and nobody stops you.

The museum itself is a separate server run by fans, where they keep copies of things people made on 2b2t. A lot of it is map art. You lay out thousands of blocks on the ground so that the in-game map of that area shows a picture.

*Close on the map art version. Show the dithering in the sky, then the flat black disc.*

Map art only has a small set of colours, so most paintings come out grainy. You can see it on the ship right next to this one. This one holds up well, I think because half of it is one flat black shape and the rest is strong orange and blue.

*Close on the small sign next to it.*

And yes, somebody rebuilt a painting called "Art Block" out of blocks. I'd like to think that was on purpose.

## Part 3: two kinds of structures

*Archive footage: flying over a player base. Farms, towers, storage halls.*

Walking through the archives, I kept seeing two kinds of structures.

The first kind is bases. Somebody travelled really far out, built a place to live, and tried to keep it secret. That's a normal Minecraft thing, you'd see the same on any server. The difference is that here a base only lasts until somebody finds it.

*The same base after the grief: craters, lava, withers.*

And sooner or later somebody does.

*Cut to spawn: a wasteland of obsidian, lava, and broken terrain.*

The second kind is the stuff you don't see anywhere else. Spawn, the highways, giant walls, pyramids, staircases that go on forever.

*A nether highway, running straight to the horizon.*

Nobody would dig a road hundreds of thousands of blocks long in their own world. People did it here because the only way to keep a base safe is to put it very far away, and then you need some way to get there.

*Back to spawn, slow pan.*

Spawn is the weirdest one to me, because nobody designed it. It's what's left after sixteen years of people building and blowing things up in the same place. It looks less like something built and more like terrain.

The way I put it to myself was this. The first kind is a game with rules being played. The second kind is its players with no rules.

Although "no rules" isn't really right. Nobody wrote any down, but if something can be found it gets destroyed, and obsidian is about the only thing that lasts. Everything that's still standing was built with that in mind.

## Part 4: the same picture

*Split screen. Left: the painting. Right: an overhead view of spawn.*

And at some point I realised I was looking at the painting again.

*Left side: highlight the landscape. Right side: an intact base.*

The landscape is the bases. It's what you get when you use the thing the normal way. It's well done and nice to look at, and you've seen it before.

*Left side: highlight the black circle. Right side: the crater at spawn.*

And the black circle is spawn.

If you took the circle out of the painting, I honestly wouldn't have stopped to look at it. And 2b2t without the anarchy is a Minecraft server with some nice bases, and there are thousands of those. In both cases the part that looks broken is the reason I care.

*Full frame: the woman facing the black. Match cut to a new player standing at spawn.*

A blank canvas has no rules about what goes on it, and neither does this server. So she's basically a new player who just joined and is standing at spawn.

*Time-lapse or montage: highways, walls, monuments.*

The difference is that the painting stops there, with her stuck. On the server people kept going, and what they ended up building in that mess is a lot weirder than any of the bases. So in a way the server shows what comes after the painting, if enough people stay at it for sixteen years.

## Part 5: the third void

*Cut to black. Hold. Then reveal that the black is a screen: an empty AI prompt window.*

Then I closed the game and opened this.

*The screen in full: "What should we work on?" and a blinking cursor.*

It's a black screen with a text box in the middle.

*Highlight each label as it's named.*

Look at what it says. "Ask anything." "Full access", so no rules here either. "No project", written twice. And then the big one, "What should we work on?", which is exactly the question you can't answer when you're blocked.

*Overlay the painting at low opacity. The prompt box lines up with where the woman stands.*

If I put the painting on top, the text box even ends up roughly where she's standing.

And this one gives you the least to hold on to. In the painting there's at least a landscape around the hole. Spawn has sixteen years of junk around it. Here there's nothing, so you have to show up with the idea already.

The model behind that box can build pretty much any software I can describe. That didn't help me come up with something to describe, the same way the view didn't help her.

And it's not just me. A lot of people have already seen this screen, or something close to it. A lot more are about to.

## Part 6: handing over the brush

*Screen recording: typing the question into the prompt.*

So I took the lazy option and asked it instead. What software would you build, if you could build anything?

*The answer appears. Let it scroll.*

It answered right away. Its first choice was a tool that traces any claim back to where it came from. The second was a simulator for everyday decisions, like whether to take a job. Both are reasonable ideas.

But that's the landscape again. It's the kind of answer you'd expect.

*Zoom on the small grey label above the answer.*

What got me was the little grey label above the reply: "Deciding how to answer a preferences question." So it didn't really have something it wanted to build. It recognised what kind of question this was and answered that.

*Zoom on the last line of the reply.*

And then the last line asks me what I wish existed. Four paragraphs in, the question is back with me.

*Split screen: the woman with her brush raised, and the finished reply.*

So it has the opposite problem from hers. She can't start. The model starts instantly, every time, and what you get is scenery. I don't know which one is worse.

*Highlight the first idea in the reply.*

But the longer I looked at the first idea, the less generic it seemed. This is a model made entirely out of things other people said. And the thing it wants to build is a tool that shows where the things people say come from. It's describing itself.

So I offered it anything, and it picked a self-portrait. Which is what Conway did. The woman in the painting is him.

## Ending: what a void needs

*Split screen: the painting and the model's reply.*

That's the thing I keep coming back to. Conway got a blank canvas and painted himself standing in front of it. The model got a blank prompt and described a tool for explaining itself. Both of them were handed nothing, and both made a portrait of themselves.

And that matters because of what's coming. In the next few months a lot more people on this planet are going to get this tool. I don't mean programmers, I mean everybody. It will build them pretty much anything they can describe. The tool itself, when I asked, wanted a portrait of itself. So what does a human build?

*Montage from the museum: highways, walls, pyramids, the meme wall.*

The only hint I have is the server, because it's the one place I know where people could build anything they wanted, for sixteen years. They built roads to get away from each other. They built walls, pyramids, and a crater. They built a wall of shrimp memes. And somebody spent hours copying a painting about having nothing to paint.

*Back in the museum. Walk up to the map art one more time.*

I don't have my own answer yet. I sat in front of that box and typed nothing, and then I made this video, which I guess is Conway's trick.

*Push in on the black disc until it fills the frame. A cursor starts blinking in it.*

But you're going to be asked the same question soon, if you haven't been already. What would you build?

*Cut to black. Credit: "Art Block" by Daniel Conway, 2022.*
